When AI meets the physical world

September 2026

Draft for review — summarised from session notes; not yet checked with the speakers.

Most of today’s conversation about AI and strategy is about software: generative models, AI agents, and what they do to organisational performance. At the Tech & AI track in Oxford I argued that we also need to understand what happens when AI moves into the physical world — when AI software meets hardware. This is what is increasingly called physical AI.

From mechanical problems to learning machines

Robotics is one of the foundational technologies of physical AI. For a long time it struggled, because so many of its problems were solved mechanically. Recent advances in software AI have changed that quickly: robots can now handle delicate tasks, such as holding a wine glass or pouring from a decanter, that were out of reach not long ago. The same shift is under way in autonomous vehicles and drones.

Why physical AI is strategically different

When AI is embedded in the physical world, its consequences become broader and more visible. That raises two strategic issues. First, firms have to work closely with a much wider set of actors — regulators, the public, and society at large — not just customers and competitors. Second, one firm’s actions spill over into other firms’ strategies, precisely because those actions are so visible and so consequential.

A case from autonomous vehicles

My own research looks at the early autonomous-vehicle industry, where two technologies — cameras and LiDAR — were competing to become the dominant way for a vehicle to perceive the road. In March 2018, both were found wanting within a matter of days. A self-driving test vehicle using LiDAR struck and killed a pedestrian, and five days later a driver died in a crash involving a camera-based driver-assistance system.

The two incidents exposed the limits of both technologies at once. What followed is the interesting part: as uncertainty rose, competitors increased their investment in searching for solutions that combined the rival technologies — what the industry calls sensor fusion. One firm’s failure redirected the innovation trajectories of others.

The question on the table

How should firms adapt and compete when AI is embedded in the physical world? The question matters well beyond innovation and nascent-industry studies. It touches corporate strategy, entrepreneurship and human-resource management too — and I hope it becomes a larger part of the strategy conversation, including at the 2027 Witan in London.
