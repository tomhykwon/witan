The opening panel at Oxford asked how strategy scholarship should change as the world it studies changes. One provocation stayed with the room all day: we have become very good at calibration, and we are quietly giving up on augmentation.

Two ways of learning

Calibration assumes we already know what matters. It asks for better evidence to sharpen estimates within a model we trust. Augmentation starts from the possibility that the model itself is wrong, and goes looking for the unknown unknowns.

The field, it was argued, has become excellent at the first. The warning signs of over-reliance are familiar: siloed disciplinary work, single methodological lenses, incremental constructs added to established frameworks, and anomalies treated as noise. The risk is perfecting a model of a world that has already moved on.

Why augmentation is undervalued

Augmentation is expensive, and its payoff is uncertain — often on a timescale that does not fit a tenure clock. Individual researchers rationally avoid it. The panel recalled James March’s case for “sensible foolishness”: exploring things whose value cannot be demonstrated in advance.

What makes it possible

History offers examples of whole systems that augmented successfully: the iron industry around Cleveland in north-east England, the Cornish pumping-engine industry, Silicon Valley. What they shared were three conditions: proximity and knowledge-sharing across organisations; collaborative norms despite competition; and ways of sharing costs so the system could do more than its parts.

The closing provocation turned the lens on ourselves. The strategy community is its own innovation cluster. Could it redesign its institutions — journals, conferences, incentives — to augment together? Gatherings like the Witan are one small attempt.
