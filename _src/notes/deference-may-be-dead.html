A decade of research on institutional complexity taught us how organisations balance competing expectations. The institutional theory track at Oxford argued that many firms now face something harder: institutional chaos, where the criteria for judging legitimacy are themselves contested and shifting.

From complexity to chaos

Complexity is balancing unions against investors. Chaos is not knowing which yardstick will be applied tomorrow. It has long been familiar in parts of the Global South, and geopolitical uncertainty and polarisation are now making it visible elsewhere.

The session drew on the case of a child-nutrition NGO in Kolkata navigating shifting rules on foreign donations and suspicion of international partners. When donations fell, it launched a commercial nutrition product, which succeeded, until donors objected to the commercial turn. The organisation repeatedly reframed what counted as “social,” spun units out and back in, and learned that what counts as proof of impact depends on who is asking.

Deference may be dead

A more provocative claim followed. Much strategy research assumes that stakeholders respect authority, institutions and evidence. That assumption is fragile. Institutional endorsement can now stigmatise rather than legitimise, and in some sectors exaggeration is not just tolerated but expected.

The silver lining

Chaos also creates openings. Where formal and informal institutions diverge, firms that break with the formal rules can be rewarded by their communities rather than punished. And firms in emerging economies have long navigated contradictory institutional logics — the West, the track suggested, has a great deal to learn from them, empirically and theoretically.
