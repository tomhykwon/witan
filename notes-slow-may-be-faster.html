Oxford 2026 · Behavioural strategy

Slow may be faster: judgment in the age of AI

September 2026

Draft for review — summarised from session notes; not yet checked with the speakers.

AI can now produce a plausible, coherent-sounding strategic narrative in seconds. The behavioural strategy and cognition track at Oxford argued that this raises the stakes for one thing above all: judgment — where it fails, how to improve it, and how to design around its limits.

More tools, more overconfidence

The sharpest line of the session: more tools do not produce better strategy; they produce more overconfident fools. AI can amplify superstitious learning — our tendency to credit our own actions for good outcomes that were really luck, “behaving like a pigeon.”

Two levers from the Carnegie tradition

The proposed principle was that slow may be faster. The first lever is strategic friction: deliberately slowing down to build the capacity to learn how to learn. The second is to learn from underdogs, not stars. What well-known stars know has usually diffused already; recent high performers are more likely to hold unusual, learnable combinations of knowledge. At a gathering of high achievers, the provocation went, you should probably learn most from the people who almost got invited.

Emotion and attention

The track also argued that grand challenges create emotional overload, not just cognitive overload, and that affective science should be built into strategy’s core theories rather than run alongside them. And the behavioural theory of the firm, which treats the decision-maker as a single agent, needs updating for groups in which each member works with an AI — or in which the AI is a member.

An agenda for five years

Integrate emotion regulation into core theory. Design organisational learning deliberately for the AI age, drawing on Simon’s design science. And study strategy the way clinicians study medicine, where breakthroughs often come from the front line and the research follows.
